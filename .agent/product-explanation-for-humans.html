PMIS is a project-planning and observability workspace. It helps a manager understand what was agreed, what is happening, what is likely to happen next, and what would change under a different plan.

01 — Why it exists

Projects rarely fail inside one tab.

Project managers reconstruct reality from schedules, messages, meetings, reports, approvals, and a browser full of open context. The burden is not simply “too many features.” It is the cost of rebuilding the project in their head every time they return.

  • The official schedule and actual work quietly drift apart.
  • Dependencies become obvious only after delay has propagated.
  • Planning decisions lose their author, reason, and approval context.
  • Prediction, commitment, and observed progress collapse into one chart.
  • Management visibility depends on another person retelling the project.

PMIS does not answer this by stripping planning down to a toy, nor by confronting the manager with every administrative module at once. It aims for calm operational depth: serious capability, disclosed progressively around the project itself.

02 — The primary workspace

The project is the interface.

The main workspace is called Timeline. It is where work identity, time, dependencies, milestones, and context remain spatially connected.

Grouped work sits on the left, the time-based plan occupies the center, and contextual task details appear alongside it. A separate task-oriented view may help with inventory, filtering, and operational triage, but it does not displace Timeline as the project manager’s primary workspace.

Users may create different perspectives, but they should not silently create different meanings.

Grouping, filtering, zooming, annotations, and saved views can remain flexible. Shared meanings—completed, blocked, critical, dependent—stay stable across the organization.

03 — The semantic model

One project. Six kinds of truth.

PMIS refuses to present planning, reality, and prediction as if they were interchangeable. Timeline may display several layers, but each keeps a distinct meaning.

Active Plan

The approved schedule currently in operation—the plan people are expected to follow.

Approved Baseline

An immutable, formally accepted reference used to understand variance and commitment.

Actual Progress

Reported or observed project events: starts, completion, blockers, and verified work.

Forecast

The expected future outcome calculated from supported inputs and dependency analysis.

Scenario

A private, non-operational what-if plan owned by one person.

Plan History

The complete, read-only record of project changes, formal moments, authorship, and effects.

Actual Progress is evidence. Forecast is inference. An Approved Baseline is a commitment. A Scenario is an argument about what could happen. Keeping these apart gives the project manager a more honest picture of the project.

04 — Plan lifecycle

Approval first. Operation second.

A what-if plan cannot jump directly into operation. PMIS separates approval from activation so legitimacy is established before a plan directs the team.

An Approved Baseline may wait for a future activation. When it is activated in an ongoing project, PMIS attempts to reconcile it with Actual Progress. Completed work and historical facts remain intact. Compatible planning changes are applied; conflicts and limitations are disclosed before the user continues.

05 — Governance

Authority should be visible.

A Project Authority Map shows who proposes, acknowledges, approves, and is informed. It explains the relationship behind a decision instead of hiding governance inside a dense permission table.

Acknowledgement means that affected leads reviewed feasibility and impact. It is not final approval and does not automatically create a veto. The sponsor remains accountable for the final decision.

06 — What-if planning

A Scenario belongs to one mind.

A Scenario is a private laboratory for a planning argument. It has one owner and one editor, preserving the integrity and authorship of that proposal.

Scenarios are unlimited and private by default. They may be shared read-only indefinitely. A viewer may create a new independent Scenario from the shared state, but the two proposals do not synchronize, merge, or rebase.

07 — Schedule intelligence

Dependencies are constraints, not decoration.

When a predecessor violates a dependent task’s minimum start date, the consequence becomes visible immediately in the Timeline.

Dependency lines are progressively disclosed: direct relationships on hover, expanded context on selection, critical chains in critical-path mode, and the complete network only when explicitly requested. This keeps the plan readable while preserving causal detail.

Forecast is calculated, not declared

Forecast uses supported inputs and dependency analysis, including PERT and CPM. Users revise estimates, dependencies, progress, blockers, or proposed dates; PMIS recalculates the expected outcome. The result is prediction—not fact, baseline, or commitment.

08 — Participation

Reporting work is not rewriting the plan.

Assignees contribute reality. Project governance determines what becomes an operational commitment.

  • Report progress, blockers, and expected delay.
  • Submit completed work for confirmation by the task’s listed approver.
  • Request a date change without silently changing the Active Plan.
  • Attach evidence and context through the task’s record.

A requested date change creates a bounded Task Change Proposal and a proposed forecast visible to the project manager or relevant supervisor. It is a focused review buffer—not a new project Scenario.

09 — Audit and memory

Every change leaves a readable trace.

Plan History is a complete, read-only, searchable record of project events and direct Timeline modifications. The application does not hide an event because it guessed that the event was insignificant.

Change Record / Schedule12 Aug · 10:42
Maya moved “Backend Integration” by three days.
  • Before: 12–15 Aug
  • After: 15–18 Aug
  • Automatic effect: QA Testing moved 3 days
  • Automatic effect: Release Forecast moved 2 days

One initiating action and its automatic downstream effects remain grouped so cause and consequence stay understandable. Corrections append a new Change Record; they do not rewrite earlier history.

Formal lifecycle events receive an automatic Key Moment label for easier navigation. An Approved Baseline is an immutable, formally approved Key Moment. Historical states are always read-only; continuing from the past creates a Scenario.

10 — The product promise

Clarity without flattening the project.

Executives receive portfolio observability: active and delayed projects, approval queues, health, risks, blockers, critical work, and upcoming milestones. Project managers receive the full planning and reasoning workspace. Assignees receive a focused way to report work and request change.

PMIS makes the project’s plan, reality, forecast, alternatives, authority, and history understandable—without pretending they are the same thing.
